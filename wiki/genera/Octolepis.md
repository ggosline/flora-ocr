# *Octolepis* Oliv.

**Family**: [[Thymeleaceae]]
**Authority**: Oliv.
**Protologue**: Oliver, Journ. Linn. Soc., Bot. 8 : 161, tab. 12 (1865)

## Diagnosis

Bentham et Hooker, Gen. Pl. 3 : 201 (1864).

= Makokoa Baill., Bull. Soc. Linn. Paris, 78 : 619 (1886).

Shrubs or small trees, sometimes large trees (Madagascar); bark traversed by numerous silvery fibres. Leaves simple, alternate, shortly petiolate, broadly ovate-lanceolate or ovate-oblong, coriaceous or not.

Plants with hermaphrodite flowers, and then frequently appearing heterostylous, or sometimes dioecious (Madagascar). Inflorescences subsessile, axillary, pauciflorous. Flowers tetra- or pentamerous, or, irregularly, both at once. Perianth comprising a calyx dialysepalous or nearly so, with 4–5 very short pieces and a corolla reduced to 4–5 petals all deeply bifid and thus transformed into 8–10 squamiform lobes entire and opposite in pairs to the sepals. 8 or 10 stamens usually hypogynous (apparently), alternating with the petaloid lobes, with free filaments. Anthers bithecate, ovate-oblong. Ovary sessile, superior, oblong, typically with 5 locules, but often having only 1 to 4. 1 ovule per locule. Style generally axillary, shorter than the ovary; stigma enlarged.

Fruit capsular, with apparently valvate dehiscence, often with 3 or 4 valves; 3 or 4 seeds (but sometimes only a single well-developed seed). Seed rather large, with albumen absent or very reduced, with thick testa, depressed longitudinally and near the base, with or without an apical appendage. Embryo straight, with two fleshy cotyledons.

Type species: Octolepis casearia Oliv., described from West Africa.

Genus known from Guinea and Liberia, to the middle Congo basin, then only from Madagascar. About ten species have been described; it seems that no more than three taxa at this rank can be retained, two from Africa appearing very closely related systematically, the third from Madagascar much more differentiated (both in its leaves and its seeds). The character of tetra- or pentamery of the flowers can only be employed by examining numerous inflorescences; as for the leaves, their dimensions and the number of their veins can vary considerably among specimens from the same collection.

In the sterile state, Octolepis can be confused with numerous shrubs, and the anatomical characters of the stems remain among the best distinguishing criteria. However, it is possible to recognize a representative of the genus Octolepis by leaves alone in relation to some other plant groups; the leaves of Octolepis frequently differ from the leaves of Carpolobia (Polygalaceae) in the type of venation; in Carpolobia, the marginal anastomoses between the strongest of the secondary veins (which are moreover few in number) are poorly marked; furthermore the tertiary network is relatively homogeneous, clearly visible on the lower surface, and forms fine meshes; in Octolepis, the lateral veins are connected to each other by some fairly strong tertiary veins forming a first network of large meshes (of the order of 1 cm per side), and the venules are only slightly visible; on the other hand, in Casearia prismatocarpa Mart. (Samydaceae), present in Gabon, the tertiary network is fairly fine, very homogeneous, regularly inclined with respect to the lateral veins, without large meshes appearing. It should also be noted that the colour and texture of the bark help to distinguish Octolepis from these different genera, as well as from certain Diospyros.
