# *Lantana* L.

**Family**: [[Verbenaceae]]
**Authority**: L.
**Protologue**: Sp . pl . : 627 (1753)

## Diagnosis

Trees, shrubs or small shrubs perennial, erect or climbing, rarely prostrate, aromatic; stems tomentose, with scabrous hairs or armed with spines. Leaves decussate or in whorls of 3 (or 4), simple, petiolate; blade dentate or crenate, often ± bullate or rugose, generally glandular-punctate. Inflorescences generally axillary, in heads or pedunculate spikes, cylindrical or subspheric; bracts linear to triangular; axis of the fruiting spike with ± scattered scars. Flowers sessile; calyx tubulate, persistent, truncate or sinuate-dentate, membranous; corolla cylindrical or enlarging above the middle, with indistinctly 2-lipped limb, with 4 or 5 unequal, obtuse or emarginate lobes; stamens 4, didynamous, inserted at the middle of the tube, included, anthers with parallel thecae; ovary with 1 carpel 2-locular, with one ovule per locule, basal or attached laterally near the base; style entire, with capitate stigma. Fruits drupaceous; mesocarp ± fleshy, rarely rather dry; endocarp hard, 2-locular or dividing into 2 one-locular pyrenes. Seeds without endosperm.

A genus of about 100 species, principally native to subtropical and tropical America, with a few from Africa to Thailand, including Nigeria, Cameroon, Gabon, Republic of the Congo, and Democratic Republic of the Congo, Central African Republic and Angola. Several species are widely cultivated as ornamental garden plants, with numerous cultivars. In Gabon, it is known from only a single introduced and naturalized species.

Notes: L. montevidensis (Spreng.) Briq. is often cultivated in gardens as a bedding plant or as ground cover.

L. camara auct., non L. Shrub or tree, 0.3 to 8 m tall, or climbing and up to about 12 m long, aromatic; stem quadrangular, covered with recurved prickles, rarely unarmed, sparsely to densely pilose, sometimes with glandular hairs, densely provided distally with sessile glands. Leaves opposite; petiole 0.7–2 cm; blade ovate to ovate-oblong, 2–12 × 2–7 cm, narrowly cuneate to rounded or sometimes subcordate at the base, acute to acuminate at the apex, coarsely to narrowly crenulate, scabrous above, with hairs tuberculate at the base, rugose, with impressed venation, pilose or glabrescent below, scabrous on the raised venation. Inflorescence in a corymbiform head, 2–5 cm in diameter; peduncle 2–12 mm, not elongating or scarcely elongating in fruit; bracts linear to very narrowly elliptic-ovate, 4–13 × 1–1.5 mm, attenuate at the base, acute to subobtuse at the apex. Flower: calyx ± 3 mm long, very slender; corolla orange, red, pink, mauve, yellow or white, often with a yellow or orange center, that of internal flowers often a different colour from external ones, sometimes changing colour with age, tube 8–13 mm long, narrow in the lower half, slightly widening above, straight to incurved, pilose outside, limb 4–9 mm in diameter, lobes oblong to rounded, up to 5 mm wide. Fruit 3–7 mm in diameter, violet or black at maturity. Distribution: of hybrid origin (see Notes); introduced and naturalized in tropical Africa, including Gabon, Republic of the Congo, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Central African Republic and Angola; in Asia; in Australia and the Pacific; in Gabon, known from Estuaire, Haut-Ogooué, Ngounié, Nyanga and Ogooué-Maritime. Ecology: often in dense scrubland in secondary forests, plantations, abandoned cultivations, roadsides and grasslands; in Gabon up to 450 m altitude, elsewhere up to 2000 m. Use: This species is cultivated as an ornamental plant for gardens and hedges, often used as firewood and fodder. In many regions, it is used in medicine to treat fevers and various ailments. In Gabon, an infusion of the leaves is used against cough, influenza, colds and headaches and the flowers are used in children's baths to combat fever. Vernacular names: mille fleurs (Fr.); lantana, red sage (Engl.); astelo noug (Cap Esterias); etchipo a tchende (Mitsogho); waga (Makina). Notes: Sanders (2006) suggested that the African, Asian and Pacific specimens well known in Africa under the name L. camara, are hybrids between several related South American species. He considered that these specimens are included in a new species with morphology and ecology different from the original parent species (including true L. camara L.) and proposed the new name: L. × strigocamara R.W.Sanders. This species is known to have numerous cultivars and hybrids among themselves. Some of these forms are sterile and harmless, while others form extensive and uncontrollable thickets, and can be toxic to livestock. For this reason the species has been declared a harmful weed in many countries. The shape and colour of the corolla vary considerably among cultivars and hybrids. The corolla can be orange, red, pink, mauve, pure yellow or white, often with a yellow or orange center; young flowers are paler and change to a deeper or different colour with age. Sometimes, within an inflorescence, internal flowers have a different colour from the external flowers of the same head.
