# *Indigofera* L.

**Family**: [[Leguminosae]] (Papilionoideae)
**Authority**: L. — Sp. pl. 2: 751 (1753)

Seventeen species.

## Diagnosis

Annual or perennial herbs or small shrubs, with **medifixed appressed hairs** —
the two-armed hairs attached at their midpoint that are the genus's most reliable
microscopic character. Stipules free. Leaves imparipinnate, sometimes
1-foliolate or simple; stipels present or absent; leaflets opposite or alternate,
the blade entire. Inflorescences generally axillary racemes, sometimes with
solitary flowers or in a head. Flowers generally rather small, under 12(–18) mm;
calyx oblique with 5 teeth, the lowest longer; corolla often red or pink, falling
quickly or sometimes persistent.

## Species in region

*I. arrecta*, *I. capitata*, *I. congesta*, *I. congolensis*, *I. conjugata*,
*I. dendroides*, *I. erythrogramma*, *I. fuscobarbata*, *I. hirsuta*,
*I. mildbraediana*, *I. paniculata*, *I. paracapitata*, *I. polysphaera*,
*I. pulchra*, *I. simplicifolia*, *I. suffruticosa*, *I. tinctoria* — 17 in all.

## Notes

**This is the indigo genus.** ***I. tinctoria*** is the classical source of
**indigo**, the most commercially important natural dye in history, and
***I. arrecta*** — Natal indigo — is African and was cultivated for the same
purpose, yielding a higher pigment content. Both are on this page. Indigo
dyeing remains a living craft tradition across West Africa, and the dye's
displacement by synthetic indigo after 1900 was one of the defining events of
the early chemical industry.

*I. suffruticosa* is the Neotropical anil indigo, naturalised here.

Several *Indigofera* species contain **indospicine** and related toxins and
cause hepatotoxicity in livestock, so the genus matters in rangeland management
as well as in dye history.
