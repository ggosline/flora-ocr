## Diagnosis

Only the principal synonyms have been mentioned. We have understood this very complex genus here in the sense in which it is generally tended to be defined today. Indeed, most species were described alternately in two, if not in three different genera which must obviously be regarded as synonymous if one wishes to accept as valid entities and of specific rank, assemblages of populations presenting from one place to another only imperceptible and continuous variations of certain characters.

These variations concern essentially the floral type (a single individual may possess both tetramerous and pentamerous flowers, and a single population appears to be able locally to show dominance of pentamerous flowers and elsewhere dominance of tetramerous flowers), the development of the internal cycle of the perianth (corolla, which may appear in the form of coloured petals as large as the sepals, or else, on a single individual or within a single population, reduced to minute glands or even absent), the nature of the ramifications and the qualities of these (woody or sub-woody savanna plants, frequently subjected to fires, and behaving as geopyrophytes).

Now, the generic distinctions were founded essentially on the floral type and on the presence or absence of petals. It is evident that these characters do not present sufficient stability for them to be taken into account at the generic level so long as it will not be possible to bring, through the play of character correlations, a confirmation of their value on the plane of fundamental systematics. Large trees, shrubs or small shrubs with erect branches often simple, often herbaceous, straight or bushy, sometimes with ericoid habit (South Africa especially) often with thickened rootstocks emitting woody or herbaceous shoots (chamaephytes). Bark variable, smooth or wrinkled, lenticellate or not, but almost always bearing long and very solid fibres. Wood with vessels frequently grouped in small islets (Leandri).

Leaves most often alternate, but sometimes opposite, the upper ones especially, in a few species (Englerodaphne), with blade of small dimensions, flat and membranous or acicular and coriaceous, variable (a few cases of heterophylly).

Notes on the distribution, ecology and probable affinities of the cited species Gnidia foliosa (Pears.) Gilg ex Engl. has been collected to date only from southern Senegal to Côte-d'Ivoire. It is a shrub of rapids margins, essentially localized on rocks, near water. Gnidia Schweinfurthii Gilg, very characteristic, belongs to a complex group essentially East African. It is a plant of savannas, reaching in northern Cameroun its western limit. Gnidia macrorrhiza Gilg (= G. mittuorum Gilg) is a savanna species with wide distribution, from Guinea to southern Abyssinia and in the Zambesian regions, to Angola. The two preceding plants are easily confused when encountered only as basal sprouts; it is likewise very difficult to distinguish them in this state from G. Buchananii Gilg, a species of Katanga and adjacent regions. Gnidia Kraussiana (Meisn.) Gilg (= Lasiosiphon Kraussianus Meisn.) is probably the African Thymelaeaceae with the widest distribution, <<<---SECTION-BREAK--->>> since it has been encountered from Guinea to South Africa, throughout the savanna zone, but also in rocky streams or small savannas in the forest zone. It is absent from Madagascar. It is also one of the most variable plants of the family, the vegetative apparatus being unrecognizable from one individual to another in neighboring localities; the leaves can be entirely glabrous, furnished with a thick, coriaceous cuticle, or else hirsute, or further softly greyish-hairy. The bracts possess the same characteristics. The dimensions of all foliar organs can vary considerably; specimens are known with very narrow-lanceolate leaves and others with leaves 4-5 cm long by 2-4 cm wide. This species can form very large bushy expanses after passage of fires. Gnidia chrysantha (Solms-Laub. ex Schweinf.) Gilg (= Arthrosolen chrysantha Solms-Laub. ex Schweinf.) is also a species with wide distribution in the savanna zone; it appears to belong to a group specially differentiated in East Africa and in the Upper Zambezi regions. Gnidia bambutana Gilg and Lederm. ex Engl., a species very localized in southwestern Cameroun, nevertheless belongs to a small group of species whose representatives exist around Lake Nyassa and in Madagascar. Gnidia apiculata (Oliv.) Gilg is a plant very characterized by its colored acute bracts. However it is very variable in its habit, and presents itself in East Africa, especially as a sub-shrub with lateral inflorescences generally all sessile; on the contrary, the plant referred to this species until now collected in Oubangui and in Cameroun possesses a very particular habit, the most inferior lateral inflorescences being borne on fairly long and ascending branchlets, giving each stem a pyramidal habit. Type of the form: Tisserant 307: Oubangui. Gnidia Claessensii Staner likewise appears to reach in Cameroun its western limit; it is a plant collected fairly near the borders of Gabon, and to the Oubangui river. It approaches systematically very closely G. microcephala Meisn. and G. stenophylla Gilg, plants distributed chiefly in East Africa, from Abyssinia to Mozambique and South African Union. Gnidia glauca (Fres.) Gilg (= Lasiosiphon glaucus Fres.) is the only truly arborescent species, able to exceed 10 m. It lives in mountain forests, the Cameroun ranges constituting its western limit. It is found especially in the high East African mountains; a vicarious species exists in the Indies. <<<---SECTION-BREAK--->>>
