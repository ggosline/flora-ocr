# *Momordica charantia* L.

**Genus**: [[Momordica]]
**Family**: [[Cucurbitaceae]]
**Protologue**: Sp.pl. 2:1009 (1753)

## Description

Plate 27(1-7); Figure 4(D, E)

Monoecious herb; stem puberulent to tomentose. Tendrils slender, simple, slender, slightly pubescent. Leaf: petiole glabrescent to hairy; blade suborbicular in outline, 5- or 7-lobed, 5–12 cm long and wide, cordate at base, membranous, glabrous on both surfaces or slightly pubescent, bright green when fresh, with lobes incised to midway along the leaf length, oval-oblong, attenuate toward base, dentate or lobulate on margins, the basal lobes being the smallest. Male inflorescence with solitary flower; foliaceous bract situated at base of pedicel, reniform to orbicular-cordate, 5–15 cm long, mucronulate at apex, with entire margin, somewhat pubescent. Male flower: pedicel slender, glabrous or slightly hairy; receptacle very small, campanulate or slightly infundibuliform; sepals narrowly elliptic-ovate, 4–6 × 2–3 mm, acute at apex; petals 10–20 × 8–10 mm, yellow when fresh, obtuse at apex, with conspicuous nerves, sometimes glabrescent, but most often covered with fine pubescence especially visible on margins, hairs pluricellular uniseriate; stamens with filament 1.5–2 mm, glabrous, anther with tripinnate locules. Female flower: pedicel 5–10 cm; ovary fusiform to ovoid, ‡longly beaked at apex, muriculate, with horizontal ovules. Fruit obloid or ±ovoid-oblong, 6–20 × 2.5–4 cm, orange at maturity, opening from apex by 3 longitudinal valves, covered with asperities. Seed 12–16 × 7–9 × 2–3 mm, almost 3-dentate at apex, pale brown.

## Distribution

Tropical and subtropical zones of the Old World, introduced elsewhere and today pantropical; widespread in tropical Africa, from Guinea to Ethiopia and south to Namibia and KwaZulu-Natal; in Gabon: Estuaire, Moyen-Ogooué, Ngounié, Nyanga and Ogooué-Maritime

## Ecology

Sandy non-flooded zones, dry degraded bush, river margins, humid sandy soils; in Gabon at 5–370 m elevation, elsewhere up to 1700 m

Use: Young fruits, leaves and shoots are eaten cooked as vegetables. The herb is laxative and diuretic, soothes irritated tissues, reduces fever, kills parasites and cleanses toxins from the intestinal system; probably also a uterine stimulant. It contains antileukemic, estrogenic and hypoglycemic chemicals. The seeds contain glycoproteins with abortive, immunosuppressive and antitumoral activity; the steroidal glucosidic charantine which has hypoglycemic activity, insulin and momordin from which an oncostatic drug is derived. The ripe fruit is a stomach tonic and induces menstruation. The fresh juice of the fruit is taken internally in the treatment of colitis and dysentery. The unripe fruit contains a peptide, similar to insulin, which has been shown to lower blood and urine sugar levels; used traditionally to treat the symptoms of diabetes, particularly late-onset diabetes. Also used to treat leprosy and malignant ulcers, stomach worms, fever and phlegm, hypertension and dysentery; also externally in the treatment of hemorrhoids, chapped skin and burns. The leaves can be used as antiseptic, blood purifier, febrifuge and vermifuge. An infusion is used in the treatment of intestinal worms, jaundice, malaria and other fevers. A decoction of leaves and stems is used to treat diabetes and high blood pressure. The juice of the leaves is applied externally to treat inflammation of the sole of the foot, scabies and skin itching. The paste of the leaves is applied as a poultice to cuts and wounds. A decoction of stems, leaves and fruits is used as a bitter tonic and to treat diabetes and cancer, also externally to treat ulcers and wounds. A decoction of flowers and leaves is used to treat hepatitis. The oil of the seeds is used to help heal wounds. The root is abortive and astringent.

## Vernacular names

concombre africain, concombre amer, margose, paroka (fr.); African cucumber, bitter melon, bitter gourd, balsam pear (angl.)
