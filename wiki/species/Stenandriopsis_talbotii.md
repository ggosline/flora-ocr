# *Stenandriopsis talbotii* (S. Moore) Heine, comb. nov.

**Genus**: [[Stenandriopsis]]
**Family**: [[Acanthaceae]]
**Protologue**: Crossandra Talbotii S. Moore, in Cat. Plants Talbot S. Nigeria 78; 140 (1913)

## Description

Plant very similar to Stenandriopsis guineensis; scarcely exceeding 20 cm in height; stem always simple; leaf blade broadly obovate, rounded and obtuse at the apex, without acumen, 7–11 × 5–7.5 cm, cordate at the base, with 3–5 pairs of lateral veins.

Bracts lanceolate, emarginate at the apex and more or less bifurcate, with a small point on the midvein at the base of the bifurcation, margins with 4–5 spines on each side in the upper part; bracteoles 5–6 mm long. Calyx approximately 1 cm long; segments subequal, entire, finely denticulate at the margins; corolla white, corolla lobes 11 × 4.5 mm; the other floral characters correspond to St. guineensis except for the pubescence of the anthers, the pollen and the stigma (see figures).

## Type

Talbot 1026, Oban, S. Nigeria (BM, holotype). S. Nigeria, Gabon

## Source note

This species has a very interesting taxonomic history, which is at the same time, in large part, that of the genus Stenandriopsis for species of the African continent. The Gabonese material preserved at the Laboratory of Phanerogams was studied minutely by L. Pierre, who attached to specimens Klaine 2198 and 2221 his notes containing analyses with very detailed descriptions, as well as two letters from C. B. Clarke, dated 22 and 24 August 1901. Concerning these two specimens, Pierre had very well recognized the position of these plants: his notes demonstrate that he had considered the specimens Klaine 2198 as very close to Crossandra guineensis Nees, and that those of Klaine 2221 represented an undescribed species. However, the description of Crossandra guineensis Nees, given by C. B. Clarke in 1899, contains a serious error: the posterior sepal was described as "2-nerved, glabrous, margin toothed, tip with 2 points", which is not at all the case in this species: the posterior sepal bifid at the apex is a generic character of Crossandra s. str., to which Cr. guineensis does not belong. The very different characters of the pollens of the plants studied and compared by Pierre had, very justifiably, led him to his own conclusions. These can be specified as follows: the material of the two Klaine numbers seemed to him to belong to a new genus Pseudocrossandra Pierre; Klaine 2198 being Pseudocrossandra Klainei Pierre and Klaine 2221, Pseudocrossandra alba Pierre. Pierre had communicated the result of his studies to C. B. Clarke, who sent him, in response, the following letter. Because of its great interest for taxonomy, this letter is copied here in its entirety.

"Herbarium, Kew, Surrey, 24 August 1901. My dear Dr. Pierre, You are right! i. e. your description of the plants Klaine n° 2198 and Klaine n° 2221 are correct, and you have emphasised the main differences. « The pollen is quite different. » Klaine n° 2198 is identic = Crossandra guineensis Nees. Klaine n° 2221 is : an example lately received here from the Gabon. — Unless we abandon altogether the views of Radlkofer and Lindau, I think it must be called : Pseudocrossandra alba, Pierre. It differs specifically from n° 2198 in the premorse bract and short bractlets, as you have pointed out; to make it a different genus is not very good botany, as it has only the rank of difference of generic value viz. the pollen. It is no defence to say that many genera of Acanths are no better separated. Believe me to be, your's with much esteem C. B. Clarke."

This response had, very probably, discouraged Pierre from publishing the results of his studies; C. B. Clarke was, with G. Lindau, the greatest authority of the time for the taxonomy of Acanthaceae. The death of L. Pierre in 1905 and of C. B. Clarke in 1906, and also the fact that the Acanthaceae of the "Flora of Tropical Africa" had just been published (1899–1900) have evidently prevented these authors from publishing an update on this subject. C. B. Clarke, who was unwilling to admit a new genus, nonetheless recognized that in the specimens Klaine 2221 it was a question of a new species.

Indeed Klaine 2198 is conspecific with Stenandriopsis guineensis (Nees) R. Benoist, and Klaine 2221 with St. Talbotii (S. Moore) Heine. The study of the pollens does not allow a generic differentiation; it is only a matter of specific characters: globular pollen in St. guineensis, pollen in the form of a small rod in St. Talbotii.

It is quite evident that the conclusions of Pierre, who had perfectly recognized the respective positions of these plants, had been made long before the description of the genus Stenandriopsis (1966) and of Cr. Talbotii S. Moore (1913). S. Moore had evidently considered his genus Stenandriopsis as monotypic and Malagasy; he never made remarks concerning the position of the Crossandra which in truth belonged to his new genus (especially his Cr. Talbotii), although he discussed in 1906 the generic differences between Crossandra Salisb. and Stenandriopsis S. Moore, without moreover entering into all the details. These differences were further discussed by K. Krause (Pflanzenfam. Nachtr. zu II–IV, 4: 286, 1915) and by Benoist in 1943. Now, in the discussion of Cr. Talbotii S. Moore, one finds the following note: "Apparently referable to the above (Talbot 1026, Oban, S. Nigeria) and differing from it only in the larger and longer-stalked leaves and the larger bracts, are the specimen of Bates, nn. 420 and 546, from Batanga. At Kew the specimens have affixed to them a MS. name of Pierre, who considered them to belong to a new genus." It is extremely curious to note that the author of the genus itself, S. Moore, was unwilling to recognize his own genus in these plants which he was discussing, and that R. Benoist, who studied the same question, likewise did not recognize the position of a considerable lot of plants from Gabon and previously studied by Pierre. The studies of Pierre, and his manuscripts attached to the corresponding specimens have thus been completely forgotten and not mentioned in the study of R. Benoist where the samples studied and annotated by Pierre did not even form part of the material representing the first species for which the true position in a genus distinct from Crossandra, the genus Stenandriopsis, had been recognized, and for which Pierre had proposed the name Pseudocrossandra which remained unpublished.

The present note does justice to the precise and conscientious work of L. Pierre on the generic position of a group of plants typically Guinean and Central African.
