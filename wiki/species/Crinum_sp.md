# *Crinum sp* . A (probablement hybride)

**Genus**: [[Crinum]]
**Family**: [[Amaryllidaceae]]

## Description

Globose bulb approximately 5 cm in diameter. Leaves in basal rosette of firm, almost succulent texture, erect, channelled, approximately 40 cm in length and 2-2.5 cm in width, with a distinct midvein and slightly denticulate margin.

Floral scape 25-45 cm long. Bracts erect, green, subtending 1-2 sessile flowers and pleasantly fragrant. Perianth tube greenish, erect, 12-14 cm in length, segments pure white, greenish at the apex, narrowly lanceolate, 6-7 × 1.5-2.5 cm. Flower shape variable within the same population, more often resembling a cup (cyathiform) rather than a bell. Filaments slightly curved in almost radial symmetry, anthers black.

Fruit green, subglobose, with a beak 3-4 cm.

These plants were collected in Cameroon on small islands near the mouth of the Nyong (Nordal 978, O, P, YA). They seem to combine characters of C. jagus and C. natans. The flower form is ± intermediate between these 2 species and shows variations within the same population. The fruits resemble those of C. jagus and the leaves share characters of the continental forms of C. natans, although firmer. Their ecology is also intermediate. At the time of collection, bulbs were growing submerged among stones with flowers and leaves above water at the same time. Similar material was collected in Gabon (Davies J.N. 326, K) although the leaves of this collection resemble more closely those of C. jagus. This specimen was wrongly attributed to C. biflorum BAK. The type of C. biflorum, from Angola, although poorly preserved, has pedicellate flowers, typically hypocratériform and cannot be conspecific.

APPENDIX

Notes on cultivated species of Amaryllidaceae.

Representatives of 3 South African genera: Amaryllis L., Clivia Lindl. and Nerine Herb. and of 4 tropical American genera: Hippeastrum Herbert, Hymenocallis Salisb., Sprekelia Heister and Zephyranthes Korovin are often cultivated in tropical Africa, including in Gabon.

Amaryllis and Nerine are close to Crinum, with which they share the following characters: solid floral scape, 2 free involucral bracts, and a capsule with globose to irregular greenish seeds. These two genera are easily distinguished from Crinum by a very short perianth tube, almost absent. Amaryllis has few flowers, pinkish, large, approximately 10 cm in length, infundibuliform; perianth segments broadly lanceolate and little reflexed. The genus is monotypic: A. belladonna L. growing naturally only at the Cape.

Nerine has smaller, more open pink flowers, numerous (5-15) arranged in an umbel. Perianth segments are almost linear, reaching 3-4 cm in length, reflexed and often undulate, crisped. N. flexuosa (Jacq.) Herb. and N. undulata (L.) Herb. are often cultivated, the former with fewer and larger flowers than the latter. Moreover there are several hybrids.

Clivia with a rhizome, 4-6 involucral bracts and red berries is probably closer to Scadoxus (or rather Haemanthus). The leaves are thick, ribbon-like and distichous. The flowers, in rich umbels, are red to orange and large (up to 8 cm). C. miniata Reg., with infundibuliform flowers and C. nobilis Lindl., with more bell-shaped flowers, are often cultivated. There are also innumerable horticultural hybrids.

Hippeastrum, a genus of the New World of 50 to 60 species, superficially resembles Amaryllis with large red infundibuliform flowers framed by 2 free bracts. The fruit, however, is different since it consists of a loculicidally dehiscent capsule with numerous black, compressed seeds.

Hippeastrum also has a hollow floral scape and small scales between the staminal filaments at the flower throat. Very often cultivated and naturalized in Gabon, H. puniceum (Lam.) Kuntze, has 2-4-flowered inflorescences and red to orange flowers, often more yellow at the centre. Hippeastrum, there is a group with trifid stigmas, one with almost capitate stigmas; H. puniceum belongs to the latter. There are innumerable horticultural hybrids, with flowers of highly variable size and colour. The genus was introduced very early as an ornamental in tropical Africa. There has been a long controversy in botanical literature, as to whether the Linnaean name (Amaryllis) should be attributed to the Cape genus or to the New World genus, here called by us Hippeastrum; LINNAEUS, in Species Plantarum (1753) was ambiguous. The nomenclature used here is now generally accepted by botanists. In horticulture and in popular tradition, Hippeastrum is still often called (Amaryllis).

Close to Hippeastrum, but with very irregular flowers, the genus Sprekelia also has a hollow floral scape, a very short tube, scales at the flower throat and capsules with black compressed seeds. The flowers are red, large and open. The 3 lower perianth segments are connivate with a tube around the style and stamens, extending in the distal part, into a prominent lower lip. The stigma is trifid. The genus is monotypic: S. formosissima (L.) Herb., originating from Central America.

Zephyranthes shares the fruit and seed characters with Hippeastrum and has a hollow floral scape and a short perianth tube. But Zephyranthes species have a tubular, fused involucre, do not have scales at the flower throat, and have a distinctly trifid stigma. The species most often cultivated in tropical Africa have pink, infundibuliform flowers: Z. rosea (Spreng.) Lindl. with flowers approximately 5 cm in length and Z. grandiflora Lindl., approximately 8 cm.

Hymenocallis superficially recalls Pancratium indigenous to the Old World with its white fragrant flowers with narrow spreading segments and a distinct staminal cup. Hymenocallis capsules contain few green, globose seeds (numerous, black, in Pancratium). The species cultivated in tropical Africa is H. littoralis Salisb. which has 6-10-flowered inflorescences. BAKER (1898), in the FTA, thinks that Hymenocallis is natural in Africa and includes H. senegambica Kunth & Bouché, distributed from Sierra Leone to Angola. Like Hippeastrum, Hymenocallis was introduced early in Africa.

KEY TO CULTIVATED GENERA (including indigenous ones)

1. Floral scape solid; seeds greenish or black, never discoid.

2. Filaments united in a staminal cup; flower pure white.

3. Inflorescence of 1-3 flowers; capsules with numerous black seeds Pancratium.

3'. Inflorescence of more than 5 flowers; capsules with few green seeds Hymenocallis.

2'. Filaments free, no staminal cup; flowers red, pink, white.

4. Rhizomes or rhizomatous bulbs; involucral bracts 4-6, free; berries with few seeds.

5. Leaves thick, ribbon-like without distinct midvein; flowers large, bell-shaped or infundibuliform; segments ovate not spreading ..... Clivia.

5'. Leaves thin, lanceolate, with a distinct midvein; flowers smaller, hypocratériform; segments linear to narrowly lanceolate, spreading ..... Scadoxus.

4'. True bulbs; 2 free involucral bracts; capsules, often irregularly dehiscent and greenish, globose to irregular seeds.

6. Flowers white or white streaked with red; perianth tube narrow, very long ..... Crinum.

6'. Flowers most often pink; perianth tube short to almost absent.

7. Flowers large, infundibuliform; perianth segments ovate little reflexed ..... Amaryllis.

7'. Flowers smaller, open, with reflexed, linear, often undulate perianth segments ..... Nerine.

1'. Floral scape hollow; seeds black, discoid.

8. Flowers strongly zygomorphic with 3 lower segments united to the tube around the stamens and style ..... Sprekelia.

8'. Flowers regular or slightly zygomorphic, infundibuliform; segments little different.

9. Involucral bracts 2, free; scales between the segments at the flower throat; stigmas capitate ..... Hippeastrum.

9'. Tubular involucre, often bifid; no scales between the filaments; stigmas distinctly trifid ..... Zephyranthes.
