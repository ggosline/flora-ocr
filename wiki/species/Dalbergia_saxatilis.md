# *Dalbergia saxatilis* Hook.f. Planche 48

**Genus**: [[Dalbergia]]
**Family**: [[Leguminosae]]
**Protologue**: Fl. Nigrit. : 314 (1849)

## Description

D. saxatilis Hook.f. var. isangiensis (De Wild.) Cronq., Fl. Congo Belge et Ruanda-Urundi 6 : 61 (1954). Liane or bushy shrub ± sarmentose, reaching 15 m in height, but often much lower, scentless, without spines, but often provided with hooked prehensile branches; bark dark brown, ± longitudinally striated, with caramel-brown exudate when cut; young branches glabrous or with tawny hairs spreading or appressed. Stipules very early caducous, lanceolate, 6–7 × 1.2–1.8 mm, acute, glabrous except for the margin sparsely ciliate. Leaf imparipinnate; petiole 0.8–4.4 cm; rachis 2.8–10.8 cm, with appressed or spreading pubescence (sometimes very sparse) similar to that of the branches; leaflets (7–)9 to 25, alternate and unequal, with petiolule of 1–3 mm, limb elliptic to obovate, symmetric, 2–7.3 × 1–3.5 cm in the upper leaflets and (0.7–)1.6–4.5 × (0.5–)0.9–2.8 cm in the lower ones, acute to slightly cordate at the base, rounded or slightly emarginate at the apex, rather thin, green or brownish when dry and often glaucous on the lower face, glabrous or pubescent on both faces, with straight fine hairs appressed or erect; lateral nerves 6 to 10, poorly marked and barely more prominent than the nervules, nervules in prominent reticulation clearly visible and generally dense, becoming looser in old leaves. Inflorescence in loose panicle, 3–20 cm in length, terminal and often also axillary, with tawny hairs spreading or appressed, sometimes sparse; bracts very caducous and usually falling when the bud opens, the lower ones broadly ovate, ± 3.5 × 2.7 mm, the upper ones elliptic or narrowly ovate, 1.3–2 × 0.5–0.8 mm, pubescent. Flower: pedicel 1–3.5 mm; bracteoles almost always caducous before flowering, narrowly elliptic, 1–1.3 × 0.3–0.5 mm, pubescent; calyx red or violaceous, glabrous or with sparse appressed hairs, tube 1.7–2.3 × 1.2–2 mm becoming ± ribbed when dry, teeth slightly imbricate and ± unequal, the upper ones connate and rounded at apex, 0.5–1.7 mm, the lateral ones rounded or more rarely acute at apex, 0.5–1.7 mm, the lower one acute at apex, 1–2.2 mm; corolla white or pale lilac with a green or yellow spot at the base of the standard, petals with claw shorter than the calyx, standard strongly bent and ± folded lengthwise, with short and thick claw of 1–1.5 mm and suborbicular limb of 4.5–6 × 4–5 mm, with slightly auriculate base and bilobed apex, wings exceeding the keel and almost entirely concealing it, with claw of 1–2 mm and very asymmetric limb of 5–7 × 2.5–3.3 mm, transversely wrinkled towards the base of the external face and ± truncate at the upper edge, keel strongly curved, with claw of 1–1.2 mm and limb of 2.8–4 × 1.5–2.7 mm, with strongly auriculate base; stamens in 2 phalanges of 5, filaments 4.5–5.5 mm, connate for ⅔ to ¾ of their length and curved in a C shape from the base; ovary 2–3 × 0.7–1 mm, pubescent at least on the margins, with ± swollen hairs at the base, with 3 ovules and stipe of ± 1.5 mm; style linear, 1–1.7 mm, with punctiform stigma. Legume with pedicel of 2.5–3.5 mm and stipe of 6–10 mm, papyraceous, flattened and ellipsoid, (7–)8.5–11 × (1.9–)2.9–4.3 cm, with reticulation flat or ± sunken (prominent only in very young legumes), glabrous except for the stipe, often glaucous, with 1 seed. Seed ellipsoid, flattened, 16–20 × 8.5–12 mm.

## Distribution

Senegal, Guinea-Bissau, Guinea, Sierra Leone, Liberia, Ivory Coast, Ghana, Togo, Benin, Nigeria, Cameroon, Central African Republic, Equatorial Guinea, Gabon, Republic of the Congo, Democratic Republic of the Congo and Angola; common and widely distributed in Gabon, recorded in all provinces except Haut-Ogooué

## Ecology

secondary forests, forest margins and regrowth, sometimes on rocky soil, often colonizes roadside embankments; 0–800 m altitude in Gabon, up to 1000 m elsewhere. In Gabon, flowering from June to February and fruiting from September to February. Uses: This plant has numerous medicinal uses in different African countries (see in particular Burkill 1995). It is used in particular against leprosy, rheumatism, leg edema and headaches (part not specified), toothaches (roots), ulcers (decoction of root powder), bronchitis (leaves in inhalation or steam baths), ear diseases (grated and boiled barks) and also to promote wound healing (leaves reduced to powder). The wood is used to make canes and tool handles. In Gabon, Raponda-Walker & Sillans (1961) report its use as a medicinal plant, but without specifying the use

## Source note

D. saxatilis is highly variable in the number, size and pubescence of the leaflets, as well as in the pubescence of the calyx (glabrous to sparsely pubescent). Forms that are very hairy, with leaflets often smaller and more numerous, are sometimes distinguished as var. isangiensis (Cronquist 1954). We have not recognized this variety here, as intermediates with the typical form are frequent. D. saxatilis is very close to D. fouilloyana and D. mayumbensis (see these species), as well as to D. lactea Vatke, a montane species present from Nigeria to East Africa, generally above 1000 m altitude. This latter species has been incorrectly reported from Gabon (Pellegrin 1949; Sosef et al. 2006) through confusion with forms of D. saxatilis with sparsely pubescent calyx. It differs from D. saxatilis in its calyx with very dense hairs completely concealing the epidermis, its inflorescences with flowers densely agglomerated at the apex of the branches, and its generally more robust dimensions. An excellent photograph of D. lactea appears in Fischer & Killmann (2008: 236). D. preussii Harms is a synonym of D. lactea (Hepper 1958) and not a variety of D. saxatilis, as Cronquist (1954) considers it. Bibliography: Adam (1971), Berhaut (1976), Cronquist (1954), Hawthorne & Jongkind (2006), Hepper (1958), Pellegrin (1949), Raponda-Walker & Sillans (1961)
