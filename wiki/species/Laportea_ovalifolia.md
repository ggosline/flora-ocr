Perennial herb with stolons, up to ± 1.25 m in height; stem erect to sometimes entirely prostrate except for the male inflorescences, somewhat woody at the base, pubescent to glabrescent, sometimes with short, ± appressed and stinging hairs, and stiff non-stinging hairs; cystoliths punctiform. Stipules linear-lanceolate, united for at least half their length, reaching ± 1 cm in length, with some stiff hairs. Leaves very variable in size and shape, generally restricted to the erect stems, but sometimes also arising on prostrate stems or even on half-buried stolons; petiole 5–10 cm, glabrous to puberulent, sometimes with a few appressed stinging hairs; blade ovate, 3.5–10 × 2–8 cm, base truncate to rounded or very broadly cuneate, apex acuminate, with the terminal tooth reaching 1 cm, margin serrate, with on each side 13 to 28 teeth of less than 0.5 cm, chartaceous, upper surface with short stiff hairs and cystoliths, lower surface with stiff stinging hairs on the veins; venation symmetrically triplinerved, the basal pair reaching the 6 to 12 apical teeth (somewhat above the midpoint of the blade), upper lateral veins in 3 to 6 pairs. Inflorescence unisexual, the male with axis generally clearly brownish-pink, differing noticeably from the stem in color and texture, axillary or arising directly from a prostrate stem or from the stolon, often on underground stolons and apparently well isolated from the leafy stems, paniculate, with total length reaching ± 50 cm; peduncle 10–20 cm; axes pubescent, somewhat watery or fleshy, the lateral ones

Male flower: pedicel up to ± 3 mm; perianth ± 2 mm in diameter; tepals 5, muticous, sometimes with a few stinging hairs. Female flower: pedicel 0.5–2.5 mm; tepals subequal, up to ± 2 mm in length, the ventral one generally shorter; ovary somewhat longer than the tepals; style with 3 unequal branches, the longest ± 3 mm. Fruit generally sessile and not reflexed, developing often underground, ovoid, laterally compressed, 3–4 mm in length, with a flattened external alate zone and one or a few ridges (up to four) surrounding on each side a central verrucose zone.

## Distribution

widely distributed in the humid dense forest zone of tropical Africa, from Sierra Leone to Cameroon, the Central African Republic, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, South Sudan, Uganda and western Kenya; southwards to Angola, Zimbabwe (one collection) and Tanzania; very probably also present in Equatorial Guinea and in the Republic of the Congo; in Gabon, very widely distributed: known from Estuaire, Moyen-Ogooué, Ngounié, Nyanga, Ogooué-Ivindo, Ogooué-Lolo, Ogooué-Maritime and Woleu-Ntem

## Ecology

humid lowland forest, often in swampy forest with Raphia and Pandanus, submontane forest, riparian forest, often on shaded river banks; able to persist in sufficiently shaded secondary forest and invasive in old plantations; in Gabon at 5–640 m altitude, elsewhere sometimes up to 2000 m in humid shaded habitat, but generally below 1500 m. Uses: The cooked leaves are consumed as a vegetable. They are also used in traditional medicine, for example as a hemostatic on cuts and wounds, as an anti-irritant, to relieve headaches. The fruit is used as an antidote to poisons. In Gabon, the cooked leaves are ingested as a remedy for stomach aches, and they are given to pregnant women once cooked with peanut

## Source note

It does not seem possible to distinguish an infraspecific taxon with only trailing stems, as proposed by Peter (1932), Hauman (1948b) and Lambinon (1959). Among the material examined, there is a complete range, from specimens with erect stems to specimens with prostrate stems and erect inflorescences arising directly from the prostrate stems or from stolons. The prostrate forms should be understood as modifications induced by the environment, growing generally in humid and shaded herbaceous formations, and should not receive taxonomic status. This species is unique among the Urticaceae and most other families in having inflorescences often arising at some distance from the assimilatory leafy stems from underground stolons, and in having female inflorescences often arising on or under the litter of the forest floor. Specimens of this species may sometimes appear to be formed of an isolated and erect male inflorescence with a fragment of an underground rooting stolon devoid of leaves, but we must consider that it must have been attached to a leafy plant. The trifid style is a character that L. ovalifolia shares only with L. interrupta; the very large achene does not exist in other species of the genus.
