# *Laportea ovalifolia* (Schumach. & Thonn.) Chew Planche 26

**Genus**: [[Laportea]]
**Family**: [[Urticaceae]]
**Protologue**: Gard. Bull. Singapore 21 : 201. (1965)

## Description

Fleurya podocarpa DC, Prodr. 16(1) : 76 (1869). Fleurya ovalifolia (Schumach. & Thonn.) Dandy, in F.W.Andrews, Flow. Pl. Anglo-Egypt. Sudan 2 : 277 (1952). Haynea ovalifolia Schumach. & Thonn., in Schumach., Beskr. Guin. pl . : 406 (1827). Fleurya podocarpa Wedd. var. mannii Hauman, Fl. Congo Belge Ruanda-Urundi 1 : 191 (1948), nom. illeg. Fleurya podocarpa Wedd. var. repens Hauman, Fl. Congo Belge Ruanda-Urundi 1 : 191 (1948), nom. illeg. Fleurya ovalifolia (Schumach. & Thonn.) Dandy var. repens Lambinon, Bull. Soc. Roy. Bot. Belgique 91 : 200 (1959). Pilea procumbens Peter, Fl. Deutsch. Ost-Afr. 2 : 126 (1932). Perennial herb with stolons, up to ± 1.25 m tall; stem erect to sometimes entirely prostrate except for the male inflorescences, somewhat woody at the base, pubescent to glabrate, sometimes with short, ± appressed and stinging hairs, and stiff non-stinging hairs; cystoliths punctate. Stipules linear-lanceolate, fused for at least half their length, reaching ± 1 cm long, with some stiff hairs. Leaves highly variable in size and shape, generally restricted to the erect stems, but sometimes also arising on prostrate stems or even on half-buried stolons; petiole 5–10 cm, glabrous to puberulent, sometimes with a few appressed stinging hairs; blade ovate, 3.5–10 × 2–8 cm, base truncate to rounded or very broadly cuneate, apex acuminate, with the terminal tooth reaching 1 cm, margin serrate, with 13 to 28 teeth on each side, less than 0.5 cm, cartaceous, upper surface with short, stiff hairs and cystoliths, lower surface with stiff stinging hairs on the nerves; venation symmetrically triplinerved, the basal pair reaching the 6 to 12 apical teeth (slightly above the midpoint of the blade), upper lateral nerves in 3 to 6 pairs. Inflorescence unisexual, the male with the axis generally clearly brownish-pink, differing noticeably from the stem in colour and texture, axillary or arising directly from a prostrate stem or the stolon, often on underground stolons and apparently well isolated from the leafy stems, paniculate, with total length reaching ± 50 cm; peduncle 10–20 cm; axes pubescent, somewhat watery or fleshy, the lateral ones reduced to glomerules reaching up to ± 1.5 cm in diameter, or sometimes with a few developed basal axes; female inflorescence in a short raceme or weakly ramified panicle, up to 5 cm long, sometimes axillary to the upper leaves (not in Gabon), but generally arising on stolons – and then the plant is generally geocarpic – ; axis slender, glabrous to puberulent, pinkish-red, bearing flowers solitary or in pauciflorous glomerules. Male flower: pedicel up to ± 3 mm; perianth ± 2 mm in diameter; tepals 5, muticous, sometimes with a few stinging hairs. Female flower: pedicel 0.5–2.5 mm; tepals subequal, up to ± 2 mm long, the ventral one generally shorter; ovary somewhat longer than the tepals; style with 3 unequal branches, the longest ± 3 mm. Fruit generally sessile and not reflexed, often developing underground, ovoid, laterally compressed, 3–4 mm long, with an external flattened winged zone and one or a few crests (up to four) surrounding on each side a central warty zone.

## Distribution

Widely distributed in the humid dense forest zone of tropical Africa, from Sierra Leone to Cameroon, Central African Republic, Democratic Republic of the Congo, South Sudan, Uganda and western Kenya; southwards to Angola, Zimbabwe (one collection) and Tanzania; most probably also present in Equatorial Guinea and the Republic of the Congo; in Gabon, very widely distributed: known from Estuaire, Moyen-Ogooué, Ngounié, Nyanga, Ogooué-Ivindo, Ogooué-Lolo, Ogooué-Maritime and Woleu-Ntem.

## Ecology

Humid lowland forest, often in swampy forest with Raphia and Pandanus, submontane forest, riparian forest, often on shaded riverbanks; able to persist in sufficiently shaded secondarized forest and invasive in old plantations; in Gabon at 5–640 m altitude, elsewhere sometimes up to 2000 m in humid shaded stations, but generally below 1500 m. Uses: The cooked leaves are consumed as a vegetable. They are also used in traditional medicine, for example as a haemostatic on cuts and wounds, as an anti-irritant, to relieve headaches. The fruit is used as an antidote to poisons. In Gabon, the cooked leaves are ingested as a remedy for stomach pains, and they are given to pregnant women once cooked with peanuts.

## Vernacular names

batsi (bapunu, bavarama, bavungu, éshira) ; bopélé (bavové) ; dipatsangwé (apin dji) ; edjamba (mpongwè) ; èdzac, kangal (fang) ; nyèngu (galoa, nkomi, orungu) ; patsango (mitsogo)

## Source note

It does not seem possible to distinguish an infraspecific taxon with only creeping stems, as proposed by Peter (1932), Hauman (1948b) and Lambinon (1959). Among the material examined, there is a complete range, from specimens with erect stems to prostrate specimens with erect inflorescences arising directly from the prostrate stems or stolons. The prostrate forms should be understood as modifications induced by the environment, generally growing in humid and shaded herbaceous formations, and should not be given taxonomic status. This species is unique among the Urticaceae and most other families by its inflorescences often arising at some distance from the assimilatory leafy stems from underground stolons, and by the female inflorescences often arising on or beneath the litter of the forest floor. Specimens of this species may sometimes appear to consist of an isolated erect male inflorescence with a fragment of an underground rootstock stolon devoid of leaves, but we should consider that it must have been attached to a leafy plant. The trifid style is a character that L. ovalifolia shares only with L. interrupta; the very large achene does not exist in other species of the genus.
