# *Trichilia rubescens* Oliv. Planche 21

**Genus**: [[Trichilia]]
**Family**: [[Meliaceae]]
**Protologue**: Fl. trop. Afr. 1 : 336 (1868)

## Description

Shrub or small tree 2–10(–18) m tall; trunk generally tortuous, 6–20(–50) cm in diameter; heartwood cream, quickly discoloring to rust, with a cedar-like odor, without exudate; young branchlets distinctly lenticellate with orange-brown corky dots, mature branchlets sometimes pustulous and often hollow. Leaf: petiole 4–12 cm; rachis 10–30 cm; petiolules 3–17 mm, those of terminal leaflets 11–26 mm; leaflets 7–15, opposite to subopposite or alternate, narrowly elliptic to elliptic or rectangular to obovate, (4–)10–25(–30) × (2–)4–7(–10) cm, obtuse to cuneiform and slightly asymmetric at the base, acuminate at the apex, generally drying reddish-brown. Inflorescence axillary or supra-axillary, 3–24(–40) cm long, strongly branched, with weakly fragrant flowers; bracts caducous. Functionally male flower: pedicel up to 1 mm; bracteoles quickly caducous; receptacle 1–2 mm long; calyx deeply 5-lobed, 1–2 × 4 mm, puberulent on the outside; petals narrowly elliptic to elliptic or obovate, 4.5–6.5 × 1.5–2.5 mm, puberulent on the outside; staminodial corona 3–4 mm long, anthers ovate, 1 × 0.5 mm; cupuliform disk, superficially 5–10-lobed, glabrous, 0.5–1 mm tall; pistillode filiform, 3–4 mm long, glabrous. Functionally female flower similar; ovary well developed, globose-depressed, 1–2 mm in diameter, 3(–4)-locular, glabrous; style 0.5–0.8 mm, glabrous, stigma pyramidal. Fruit obovoid to subglobose, (2–)3(–4)-lobed, 1–2 cm in diameter, glabrous, dehiscent by 2–3(–4) transversely ridged valves.

G: W.J. de Wilde c.s.1532; K–N: Breteler 1756; P: Dale U-696). Drawing by Ike Zewald, Naturalis Biodiversity Center, Leiden (©).

Seed 10 × 5–7 mm, with dark brown, shiny testa, the half modified into a fleshy scarlet sarcotesta.

## Distribution

Nigeria, Cameroon, Equatorial Guinea (including Bioko), Central African Republic, Gabon, Republic of the Congo, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Angola (Cabinda), Uganda and Tanzania; in Gabon, fairly common

## Ecology

primary and secondary dense humid forest, semi-deciduous forest and associated forest galleries; in Gabon, up to 900 m altitude, elsewhere up to 1500 m

## Uses

Decoctions of bark are used in traditional medicine, those of roots serve as vermifuge, abortifacient and for making arrow poison. Leaf extracts have shown antimalarial activity. B: Lemmens (2008b), Staner & Gilbert (1958), Styles & White (1991), de Wilde (1968) Bibliography
