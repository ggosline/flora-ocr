# *Capsicum frutescens* L. Planche 30

**Genus**: [[Capsicum]]
**Family**: [[Solanaceae]]
**Protologue**: Sp. pl. 1 : 189 (1753)

## Description

Perennial herb, erect, or small shrub, up to 2.5 m tall; stems spreading, angular, sparsely pubescent with simple glandular or non-glandular hairs, rapidly glabrescent. Leaf: petiole (0.5–)1–2(–4) cm, pubescent; blade elliptic-ovate, sometimes ovate, (1–)5–12 × (1.5–)2–5.5 cm, attenuate and decurrent at base, acuminate at apex, concolorous, sparsely pubescent with simple hairs on both surfaces, with pubescent domatia beneath. Inflorescence with 2 to 4 flowers or more rarely with solitary flower. Flower: pedicel 7–18 mm, erect and curved, thickened and elongating to 3 cm in fruit, glabrescent; calyx campanulate, 1.5–3 mm long, glabrescent, with truncate apex or undulate margin; corolla stellate, 6–10 mm in diameter, white to greenish, rarely yellow, tube 1–2 mm long, lobes spreading or reflexed, 2–5 × 1–3 mm, puberulent at apex; stamens glabrous, with filament 0.5–1.5 mm, anther 1.5–2.5 mm long, blue to purple; style 3–4.5 mm, glabrous. Fruit most often in pairs and erect, ovoid to narrowly conical 0.8–2 × (0.3–)0.5–0.6(–1) cm, red, sometimes orange at maturity, glabrous. Seed ovoid, discoid or reniform, 3–3.5 × 2–3 mm, yellow to yellow-orange.

## Distribution

Native to South America and naturalized throughout the tropical regions of the globe and in south-western Europe; present throughout sub-Saharan Africa; in Gabon, known from Estuaire, Nyanga, Ogooué-Ivindo, Ogooué-Maritime and Woleu-Ntem.

## Ecology

Common weed in secondarized habitats (abandoned cultivations, forest edges and roadsides), often in shade; in Gabon, at 30–700 m altitude, elsewhere up to 1250 m. Uses: The fruits (peppers) are most often used fresh, dried or processed as a condiment to color and enhance the flavor of dishes. Young leaves are consumed as a seasoning vegetable or as spinach. In therapeutic use, pepper is applied externally as a rubefacient for rheumatism, as well as to treat joint and muscle pain. For internal use, peppers are ingested fresh or as a macerate to treat dental infections, cure sore throat and combat abdominal pain, neuralgia and headaches. Peppers are also consumed to prevent the onset of cardiovascular disorders, hemorrhoids and varicose veins. Capsaicin, the active compound contained in peppers, stimulates the mucous membranes of the mouth, stomach and intestines. The consumption of pepper thus has a slight laxative effect, contributes to increasing salivation and perspiration and facilitates digestion.

## Source note

C. frutescens is the most commonly harvested Capsicum species in Africa. It has been suggested that this species was first domesticated in the Panama region before being introduced to Central and South America and more recently to the rest of the world (De Witt & Bosland 1996). Differentiating sweet pepper (C. annuum L.) and C. frutescens from herbarium specimens is particularly difficult and the delimitation of these two species is subject to controversy. Nevertheless, it is possible to differentiate two distinct taxonomic entities at the level of inflorescence, flowers and fruits. Flowers are generally solitary in C. annuum whereas they are most often in 2 to 4 in C. frutescens. Fruits are always solitary, often pendant and of relatively large size (2.5–5 × 1.5–2 cm) in C. annuum; they are generally in pairs, erect and of smaller size (0.8–2 × 0.3–1 cm) in C. frutescens. After a thorough re-examination of the Capsicum collections available for Gabon, it appears that only C. frutescens is actually present in the collection; C. annuum, the most widespread and cultivated species of the genus in the world, is comparatively poorly represented in the herbarium material from Africa (Edmonds 2012).
