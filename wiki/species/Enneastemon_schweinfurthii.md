## Description

(PL. 44, p. 247)

- Robson in Exell et Wild, Fl. Zamb. 1, I: 129, tab. II B (1960). — Paiva, Mem. Soc. Brot. 19: 59 (1966).

Taken in the broad sense, this species is widely distributed in Africa, from Cameroon to Kenya in the east and to Rhodesia in the south.

Described from a single flowering specimen from Congo-Kinshasa, from the Ubangi-Uele region, Popowia Schweinfurthii = Enneastemon Schweinfurthii was characterized by the form of its stamens with the connective enlarged in the shape of an elongated cup, narrow filament and short anthers placed externally beneath the connective. The number of these stamens, reduced to 6 on the type described by Engler and Diels, seems in fact very exceptional and extremely rare in all the material subsequently referred to this species. It cannot therefore be used to distinguish E. Schweinfurthii from the three related species described subsequently: E. Seretii (De Wild.) Rob. et Ghesq., E. affinis Rob. et Ghesq. and E. angolensis Exell, of which only the type of the latter bears fruit.

Several characters have been used to separate these 4 species:

The STAMENS: Their number is generally 9, a number characteristic of the genus, and can vary between 8–10, but the figure of 6 is quite rare, as we have noted above.

The shape of the connective has been described as acetabulate, pyramido-cuneate, triangular inflexed toward the center of the flower; examination of all the types leads to defining a form of connective stable and identical for these 4 species; the connective is strongly enlarged above a relatively narrow filament, in the shape of a thick, elongated cup, with inflexed edges, slightly extended toward the center of the flower. The figures given by the authors of these different species present moreover only details of interpretation that are found in each of the species, according to the specimens studied.

The INFLORESCENCES: All described as axillary, it is observed on abundant material that they can be axillary or supra-axillary.

Solitary or geminate in E. Schweinfurthii, they become, in the original descriptions, 1–3-fasciculate in E. angolensis, 3–6-fasciculate in E. affinis, 3–7-fasciculate in E. Seretii. All the specimens actually present inflorescences of the same type: these are fascicles arising from bud series, simple or sometimes ± abnormal, taking the appearance of small cymes by short fasciation of the pedicels. Two groups of specimens can only be distinguished: some bearing generally solitary or geminate flowers on which however some fascicles of 3–4 flowers are found; the others having on the contrary most generally fascicles of at least 3 flowers, up to 7-flowered, on which it is nonetheless not rare to encounter solitary or geminate flowers.

Perhaps one can interpret the preponderance of one or the other of these two dominant types by the action of certain ecological factors.

The COROLLA PREFLORATION: The greater or lesser free surface, left at the base of the external petals, according to their degree of connivence at the apex, has been invoked by Robyns and Ghesquiere to separate E. affinis from E. Seretii. It is in fact quite variable but very clearly a function of the development of the flower or the floral bud.

The LEAVES: Narrowed and obtuse at the base, acuminate at the apex in E. Schweinfurthii, they become obtuse at the base, obtusely and shortly acuminate in E. affinis, rounded to subcordate at the base, obtuse or shortly acuminate at the apex in E. Seretii and E. angolensis.

To each of these leaf forms, whose variation is very gradual in the group, correspond several types of mericarps. Should one then take into account leaf polymorphism or variation of the fruits?

The FRUITS: Only the mericarps of E. angolensis have been described from the type: like all the others, they are moniliform, but the articles are globose and smooth.

The other forms of mericarps are sometimes very constricted between the articles narrowly cylindrical or ellipsoid, smooth, sometimes ± narrowed between the articles globose or subglobose, smooth or slightly wrinkled, sometimes verrucose, slightly or strongly constricted between the oblong articles. Still it must be added that the young fruits present many intermediate forms.

All these fruits have hitherto been referred to the four species studied in such a way that it is impossible to clearly define the type of mericarps of each of them, the different authors having, it seems, sometimes considered the fruit, sometimes the leaves.

No constant correlation existing between the different characters studied above, it seems to us difficult to establish with certainty several species in this group. It seems that we are in the presence of a species widely distributed in tropical Africa and extremely polymorphous, as are many lianes. It would no doubt be appropriate to combine these taxa into a single widely defined species, but the fruits offering aspects sometimes so different, it seems to us preferable, for the moment, to propose three varietal forms based on the mericarps.

Scrambling shrub or liane reaching 2–15 m in height; branchlets densely ferruginous tomentose becoming glabrous. Leaves with petiole 3–10 mm long, appressed-pubescent; limb papyraceous to subcoriaceous, discolorous, obovate to elliptic-oblong, (5) 7.5–19 (26) cm long, (2.5) 3.5–8 (10.5) cm wide, cuneiform, obtuse, rounded or subcordate at the base furnished with two small black linear glands, acuminate or obtuse and shortly acuminate at the apex; upper surface green, glossy, glabrous, lower surface glaucous, appressed-pubescent. Lateral veins 8–15 pairs, ± prominent on the lower surface.

Flowers axillary or supra-axillary, solitary, geminate, or in linear fascicles 3–7-flowered with pedicels sometimes fasciate, giving the appearance of small cymes; pedicel slender, (0.6) 1–2.5 cm long, appressed-pubescent rufous. Bracteoles small, ovate, 0.5–1 mm long, appressed-pubescent. Sepals united into a small cup, lobes broadly triangular-ovate to subcircular, 1–2 mm in diameter, acute or rounded at the apex, appressed-pubescent on the outside, glabrous on the inside. Petals fleshy, yellowish or brownish, appressed-pubescent on the outside, tomentose on the inside; the external ones obovate to subcircular, 5–8 mm long, 4–6 mm wide, attenuate at the base; the internal ones thicker, shorter and narrower, triangular at the apex, distinctly narrowed at the base (2 mm wide). Stamens (8) 9 (10), about 2 mm long; filaments relatively narrow; connectives strongly enlarged in an elongated cup, with inflexed edges, ± extended toward the center of the flower; anthers short, extrorse, placed beneath the connective. Carpels 6, 2.2–2.5 mm long, ovaries ovoid, densely rufous-hairy, styles short, cylindrical, slightly bifid.

Fruits with pedicel 1–3 cm long, ± appressed-pubescent; mericarps cylindrical moniliform, shortly stipitate, stipes 3–7 mm long; articles ± separated, narrowly cylindrical to ellipsoid, globose to subglobose, oblong, smooth, wrinkled or strongly verrucose, the terminal one apiculate.

## Discussion

We have referred this form of mericarps to the typical variety, because of the similarity of the leaves that generally accompany them throughout the material.

Most widely distributed variety, from Sudan to Nyassaland and Northern Rhodesia in East Africa, to Congo-Kinshasa and Angola (?).

Defined by mericarps with globose articles smooth or barely wrinkled, this variety is very polymorphous. The specimens from the Central African Republic have clearly obovate leaves, obtuse at the base and attenuate-acuminate at the apex, while the rest of the material presents leaves rounded to subcordate at the base, obtusely and shortly acuminate at the apex. It is possible moreover that certain flowering-only numbers from Congo-Kinshasa belong to var. Tisserantii.

The specimens from C.A.R., cited by Tisserant and Sillans (l.c.: 334) as E. Barteri, are flowering-only, but can very probably be brought close to the fruit-bearing numbers cited above; the stamens do not have the wide filament of those of E. Barteri.

Shrubs or lianes with simple hair indumentum. Flowers axillary or extra-axillary, solitary, 2–4-fasciculate or in long capillary racemes. Buds subglobose; bracteoles present. Sepals 3, triangular-ovate, united at the base, valvate. Petals 6, more rarely 4–5, valvate, uniseriate in the bud and in the open flower, free, thick, contiguous from base to apex. Stamens 6–17, in one or two whorls, all fertile or some fertile alternating with others sterile; filaments ± long; thecae extrorse or introrse; connectives developed and truncate above the anthers, ± prominent toward the outside. Carpels 7–24, ovaries ellipsoid-oblong, hairy; styles distinct or not, stigmas ± bilobed. Ovules 1–4, basal erect or lateral ascending.

Fruits with mericarps shortly stipitate; articles ellipsoid-oblong, constricted between the seeds. Seeds 1–2 (3–4), ellipsoid-oblong.

Type species: M. congoensis Baillon.

Small African genus comprising 4 species, limited to the dense humid forests of Gabon, Congo and northwestern Angola; two of them are known only from Gabon.

Very close to the African genera Enneastemon Exell and Gilbertiella Boutique, it differs from them essentially by the prefloration of the petals in the bud, uniseriate and entirely contiguous from base to apex. In the two other genera, the petals are uniseriate in the open flower, and biseriate in the bud, the large ones almost entirely covering the small ones. The genus Monanthotaxis could be the last link in an evolutionary chain from the large group of Popowia, whose petals have a prefloration always biseriate.
