# *Schwenckia americana* L. Planche 33, Figure 7(D, E)

**Genus**: [[Schwenckia]]
**Family**: [[Solanaceae]]
**Protologue**: Gen. pl., ed. 6 : 567 (1764)

## Description

Annual or perennial herb, erect, up to 70 cm tall; stems erect, sometimes prostrate, pilose, with a mixture of long and spreading hairs, and short and appressed hairs, glandular or not. Leaf: petiole (0–)1–5(–8) mm, pubescent; lamina ovate to elliptic, 0.5–2.5(–3) × 0.5–1 cm, becoming smaller and sessile distally, cuneate to attenuate at the base, obtuse or sometimes acute at the apex, pilose especially beneath on the veins. Inflorescence terminal or axillary, with cymes grouped into racemes or panicles, with flowers solitary or in pairs; axes glabrous to sparsely pilose. Flower actinomorphic to slightly zygomorphic; pedicel 1–3 mm, glabrous to sparsely pilose; calyx 2.5–4 mm long, glabrous to pilose with glandular hairs, with triangular lobes, often unequal, 0.5–1 mm long, accrescent to 3 mm on the fruit; corolla narrowly tubular, white, yellowish-green, pale blue to violaceous, tube straight or slightly curved at the base, 5–8 mm long, 0.5–1.5 mm in diameter at the apex, deeply nerved, glabrous externally, with lobes 0.5–1 × 0.3–1 mm, alternating with 2 to 5 glandular appendages, equal or unequal, linear to spatulate; stamens 2, equal, inserted in the lower third of the tube, filament pubescent, anther 0.5–1 mm long, pale yellow; staminodes 3, equal; disk 0.5–1 mm in diameter, glabrous; ovary ellipsoid; style 4–6 mm. Fruit spheric to ovoid, 3–5 × 2.5–4 mm, smooth, glabrous, pale brown, lower part covered by the accrescent calyx. Seeds cubic, 0.1–0.8 × 0.1–0.8 mm, verrucose, brown.

## Distribution

Native to tropical America, introduced to Africa probably accidentally in the early nineteenth century; now naturalized throughout tropical Africa; in Gabon, known from Estuaire, Haut-Ogooué, Moyen-Ogooué, Ngounié, Nyanga, Ogooué-Ivindo and Ogooué-Maritime.

## Ecology

Adventive, locally abundant in disturbed habitats (dumps, cultivated and abandoned fields, secondary forests), in savannas and grasslands; in Gabon at 0–630 m altitude, elsewhere up to 1300 m. Uses: In West Africa the aerial parts are generally used to treat affections of infants and young children. Various parts of the plant (roots, leafy stems, sap) are used to treat respiratory diseases (cough and asthma), stomach complaints, headaches, convulsions with fever, aphthous ulcers, skin diseases (measles, edemas and chickenpox) as well as for various curative and preventive uses (antidote, teeth whitening, laxative or female infertility).
